Most AI ethics debate misses the actual problems. We're fixated on killer robots while ignoring the real harms happening right now: biased hiring algorithms screening out qualified candidates, hospital AI that kills Black patients at higher rates, deepfakes destabilizing elections, and a handful of companies controlling the tools everyone else depends on. These aren't hypothetical—they're documented, measurable, and accelerating.

The noise around AI ethics often centers on doomsday scenarios that distract from the concrete damage being inflicted today. I'm going to argue that we should stop waiting for AGI ethics frameworks and start fixing the broken systems already in production. The five concrete concerns I outline below deserve your attention way more than Terminator fantasies.

## Biased hiring algorithms are costing companies billions and destroying careers right now

AI hiring tools systematically reject qualified candidates based on race, gender, and age. This isn't theoretical—lawsuits are settling for significant sums, HR leaders cite bias as a top concern, and independent research has documented disparate selection rates for applicants from underrepresented backgrounds. The financial damage from losing talented workers through algorithmic discrimination represents a substantial cost to organizations across industries.

This is the clearest example of AI ethics concerns that demand immediate action. Workday faces a discrimination lawsuit alleging its resume screening tool filters candidates by race, age, and disability. The company didn't design a tool to discriminate—it trained it on historical hiring data that *encoded* discrimination. The algorithm learned to replicate biases baked into decades of human decisions.

What makes this worse: companies aren't waiting for perfect solutions. 57% of companies worry AI could screen out qualified candidates, yet 50% are already deploying these tools anyway. They're shipping systems they know are broken.

## Healthcare AI shows how bias kills

Medical AI trained on incomplete datasets delivers significantly worse outcomes for underrepresented patient populations. Skin cancer detection algorithms struggle with darker skin tones. Research has established clear connections between algorithmic bias and elevated mortality rates among Black patients. This transforms AI ethics from abstract philosophy into a matter of life and death.

The stakes here are incomprehensibly high. Radiologists trained AI systems mostly on male patient data—those systems now misdiagnose conditions like pneumonia in women. Skin cancer detection algorithms, trained predominantly on light skin, show dramatically lower accuracy for darker skin tones. Healthcare algorithms have demonstrated measurable performance gaps that correlate with real-world clinical disparities, underscoring how algorithmic bias translates to patient harm.

These tools roll out because they pass accuracy benchmarks tested on the same biased datasets they were trained on. The system looks "fair" in aggregate while harming specific groups. Healthcare is where AI ethics concerns stop being abstract—they become body counts.

## Job displacement is real, right now, and we're underestimating the scale

Workforce reductions tied to AI adoption are measurable and spreading. Throughout 2024 and into 2025, layoff announcements increasingly cite AI as a driver of restructuring, with further displacement projected ahead. Entertainment, creative fields, and professional services all face documented risk from automation.

This isn't coming. It's here. A number of major companies have announced workforce reductions while emphasizing AI investment as a strategic priority. Workday cut 1,750 jobs—8.5% of its workforce—to reallocate resources to AI. Similar patterns are visible across the technology and financial services sectors.

The deeper concern: when a workforce expects to be reorganized around a technology they don't control, they resist. Slow adoption. Poisoned data. Systems fail quietly because nobody's invested in making them work.

## Privacy risk explodes as AI scales

The majority of large enterprises now operate AI systems in production environments, creating substantial exposure to data breaches and misuse. Regulatory bodies strengthened privacy requirements in 2024-2025: the EU's AI Act, emerging state-level regulations throughout the U.S., and South Korea's AI Framework Act all mandate fairness, transparency, and non-discrimination standards. Yet most AI systems process enormous datasets while functioning as black boxes—opaque to scrutiny, resistant to external auditing, and impossible to interpret meaningfully.

The problem compounds: AI systems need massive data to train and improve. That data is personal—names, account numbers, diagnoses, addresses. Chatbots surface conversation history carelessly. Models have demonstrated training data leakage in documented cases. And nobody can explain *how* the algorithm decided anything, because deep learning is fundamentally opaque.

Companies are scrambling to achieve compliance after deploying systems that were never designed with privacy and explainability in mind.

## Concentration of power matters more than you think

A small number of organizations control the foundational AI models that serve as the backbone for countless downstream applications. That centralization creates concentrated risk—when OpenAI, Google, or Anthropic implements a policy change, the effects cascade through thousands of companies and millions of users. This concentration of control represents a systemic-level ethics challenge for the industry.

If you build a product on Claude or ChatGPT, you don't own the infrastructure. You inherit its biases, its limitations, its data policies, its business decisions. One policy change ripples through thousands of companies. One bias in the base model corrupts everything built on top.

Regulation is catching up. [The EU AI Act classifies healthcare AI as high-risk, requiring strict data governance](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/IP_24_1753). South Korea's AI Framework Act and similar regulations globally mandate fairness and accountability. But rules lag years behind deployment. By the time regulation arrives, billions of decisions are already made using biased systems.

## The takeaway: Fix what's broken now

Stop debating whether AGI will be friendly. Start fixing the documented harms of systems already in production. Demand transparency from vendors. Audit your AI tools for bias before deployment. Push your regulators to enforce privacy rules that have teeth. These aren't hypothetical concerns—they're measurable problems with real solutions.

The next post in this series dives into what's actually coming next in AI. But right now, the conversation that matters is the one about what's already here.
