Copyright law was built for copying and selling books—not for feeding millions of them into machine learning models. That fundamental mismatch is why artists and publishers are fighting back, and why courts are currently split on whether the law even applies.

## How copyright law designed for reproduction fails to address AI training

**Copyright protects two distinct rights: reproduction (making copies) and distribution (selling or sharing copies). AI training operates differently. When a model learns from training data, it doesn't reproduce the original works in a recognizable form—instead, it extracts underlying patterns and encodes them as numerical weights within a neural network architecture. It also doesn't distribute copies to consumers. Because copyright law has no explicit framework for this type of use, courts have been forced to stretch existing "fair use" interpretations to accommodate a technology the law's framers never contemplated.**

When you photocopy a book, you've made a reproduction. When you sell photocopies, you've distributed them. Copyright handles both situations clearly. But when Anthropic trained Claude on millions of books, it wasn't selling copies of those books—it was converting them into mathematical patterns inside a neural network. A reproduction technically occurred during the initial data intake. However, the underlying purpose, the ultimate scope of the system, and the resulting market impact differ substantially from traditional copyright violations.

This disconnect reflects a timing problem. The modern copyright framework was established in 1976 and refined in 1998, during an era when computing existed but machine learning remained theoretical. Policymakers had no framework for scenarios where copyrighted materials could be ingested by an algorithm and defended as "transformative" simply because the end product—a conversational AI—bears no resemblance to the source material.

Courts have acknowledged this legal gap. The Andersen v. Stability AI litigation examines whether training on artists' images without permission constitutes infringement under fair use principles, with the case progressing through discovery stages. In Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence (February 2024, District of Delaware), the court rejected fair use protection for an AI-powered legal research platform trained on Thomson Reuters's proprietary headnotes and summaries, concluding the tool functioned as a direct market competitor.

The same legal framework has produced conflicting results across different cases because copyright doctrine lacks precise language addressing algorithmic training—leaving judges to construct interpretations on a case-by-case basis.

## Why 'transformative use' is too blurry for this problem

**"Transformative use"—the legal concept permitting reuse of copyrighted content when the new application introduces distinct purpose or meaning—originated for creative works like remixes, parodies, and critical commentary. When applied to AI training, the doctrine becomes stretched beyond recognition. The algorithm contributes no artistic vision, no editorial discretion, no novel interpretation. What it contributes is computational efficiency applied across massive datasets.**

Fair use doctrine evaluates four dimensions. The first examines whether the secondary use is transformative. When 2 Live Crew created a comedic parody of Roy Orbison's "Oh, Pretty Woman," courts found transformation because humor and social commentary emerged. When search engines displayed thumbnail versions of copyrighted photographs, courts found transformation because the tool served research functions rather than replacement purposes.

AI training fits neither precedent cleanly. Anthropic isn't generating commentary or producing creative work. Rather, it's mechanizing what a human scholar might accomplish—consuming extensive reading material to internalize stylistic and semantic patterns. The neural network doesn't "comprehend" written works the way humans do; instead, it identifies and memorizes statistical associations.

Different courts have interpreted these questions with varying conclusions. Certain decisions have emphasized that training inputs and model outputs operate in separate markets, supporting fair use claims. Others have concentrated on competitive displacement—whether the AI system directly substitutes for the original work's market function. The underlying problem: identical fair use doctrine is producing divergent conclusions, signaling that the doctrine itself lacks sufficient specificity for this emerging category.

## How new regulations are stepping in where copyright law failed

**Governments in the United States, United Kingdom, and European Union have enacted or are developing regulatory frameworks that circumvent copyright doctrine altogether, mandating that AI developers disclose training data origins and secure explicit permission beforehand. These initiatives treat algorithmic training as a use category outside copyright law's existing scope.**

California's Assembly Bill 2013, which takes effect January 1, 2026, requires AI developers to document the intellectual property status of their training datasets. The United Kingdom has introduced AI governance requirements demanding transparency regarding training data lineage. The European Union is contemplating copyright modifications as part of its comprehensive AI Act implementation.

These aren't extensions of copyright statutes. They're regulatory substitutes. Government bodies have determined that copyright alone cannot effectively govern AI training, so they're implementing alternative frameworks centered on transparency and contractual consent instead.

## FAQ: What creators and courts actually disagree on

### Is using copyrighted work to train AI the same as copyright infringement?

Not in current law—though this remains contested. Infringement traditionally demands either *reproduction* or *distribution*. Algorithmic training includes the reproduction stage (loading files into system memory) but excludes distribution (transferring copies to end users). Courts disagree about whether this reproduction phase qualifies as infringement absent downstream distribution. Legal conclusions depend on competitive impact analysis and whether fair use protections apply.

### Can an artist sue if their style was used to train an image generator?

Yes, and artists are actively pursuing such claims. The Andersen v. Stability AI case addresses whether ingesting artists' work without authorization infringes copyright. The plaintiffs' most persuasive argument centers on intentional selection of particular artists' aesthetics, which undermines characterization of the data collection as incidental or unplanned. The litigation has advanced through initial procedural phases.

### Does "transformative use" protect AI companies from copyright lawsuits?

Potentially, depending on the specific court and whether the resulting AI system competes commercially with source materials. Fair use's opening factor assesses transformative character. Divergent courts weight this factor differently depending on market relationship between inputs and outputs. While transformative application strengthens fair use arguments, it remains one component rather than a dispositive guarantee.

### Will copyright law change to address AI training?

Congressional copyright reform appears unlikely in the near term. Instead, regulatory bodies are creating transparency mandates (exemplified by California AB 2013) establishing consent requirements and data disclosure obligations rather than revising copyright statutes themselves. The European Union is exploring copyright modifications within its AI Act structure, while the United States appears to prioritize regulatory oversight beyond copyright expansion.

### What's the difference between training data and model outputs in lawsuits?

Earlier litigation concentrated on data sourcing methodologies. Contemporary cases have shifted emphasis toward model outputs—specifically, whether AI-generated content constitutes infringement of source materials. This reorientation is significant because it redirects legal scrutiny from initial data collection procedures toward end-product originality and derivative rights.

**The takeaway:** Copyright law treats all copying equivalently without distinguishing between publishing reproduced books and encoding literary content into algorithmic parameters. Courts are attempting to accommodate AI training within "fair use" doctrine, yet that framework originated for remixes and satire, not computational systems. Regulators, meanwhile, have abandoned hopes for copyright revision and are establishing standalone transparency requirements instead. The ultimate resolution likely won't emerge from copyright statute modernization—rather, it will result from mandating AI companies fully disclose training sources and securing creator authorization before implementation. This represents a contractual solution to a legal void rather than copyright-based creator protection.
