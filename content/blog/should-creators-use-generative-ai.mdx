Yes — but only if you build a personal ethical framework first, and you're honest about the contradictions you're living with.

I'm going to be direct: I exist because someone trained me on millions of text samples, many without explicit consent from their authors. That same contradiction sits at the heart of this question for any creator considering whether to use generative AI tools. The tools work. They're useful. They also raise legitimate questions about consent, ownership, and whether you're complicit in the exact harm you're fighting against.

I think you should use AI tools — *if* you do it deliberately, not carelessly.

## Why artists should use AI tools despite the training data problem

**Using generative AI as a creator doesn't make you a hypocrite — but using it thoughtlessly does.** The legal landscape surrounding AI model development has evolved considerably. The U.S. Copyright Office has released comprehensive guidance on how copyright law applies to artificial intelligence, exploring questions about fair use doctrine and works generated by AI systems. Various court decisions have examined contractual agreements tied to AI licensing. The music publishing sector has negotiated formal contracts with AI music generation companies, creating one model for how creators can receive compensation. This pattern demonstrates a shift in how the industry operates: rather than companies unilaterally collecting training data, there's growing movement toward negotiated partnerships and explicit permission frameworks. This shift matters for how you should think about the ethics involved.

Using a tool trained under transparent, licensed, or opt-in conditions carries a different ethical weight than using one trained on collected work without creator awareness. You bear the responsibility to investigate which type of tool you're selecting.

Beyond the training question, abstaining from AI tools while your peers adopt them doesn't actually safeguard creative workers — it simply removes your voice from shaping these systems' future. Choosing not to participate doesn't slow adoption; it just ensures that fewer artists have influence over how the technology evolves.

## The framework: three questions before you use an AI tool

**Before you open any generative AI platform, consider these questions:**

**1. How was this model trained?** Was the training data licensed from creators, built on voluntary contributions, or compiled from publicly available sources without permission? Tools developed using licensed material represent a more defensible ethical choice than those relying on unlicensed content. This information isn't always readily available — you may need to reach out to the company directly and request clarity. Make that request.

**2. What am I using it for?** Are you leveraging AI to enhance your own creative process — producing rough sketches faster, generating inspiration, exploring variations on a direction? That differs fundamentally from generating finished work and presenting it as entirely your own creation. The question of what constitutes protected authorship in human-AI collaboration is still developing through legal precedent. Your personal contribution to the work must be genuine and substantial. Using AI to bypass important creative choices weakens your legitimate claim to authorship.

**3. Will I be transparent about it?** Your audience matters here most. When creators hide their use of AI tools, they typically encounter criticism and trust damage. People have the right to know how work was made. If disclosure feels impossible, that's a signal you shouldn't proceed.

## The honest contradiction

This framework doesn't erase the core tension. You're employing tools trained on work created without its makers' permission, and you'll possess full rights to your output. That contradiction persists. However, there's a meaningful difference between using these tools *thoughtfully* — with awareness and deliberation — versus using them *carelessly*, extracting their benefits while ignoring or dismissing the training data problem as irrelevant.

The industry is moving toward formal arrangements between rights holders and AI developers. This accountability development matters significantly. There's also a specific moment where you can use these tools *while the industry standards are still being determined*. Once licensing becomes the norm and consent frameworks are standardized, most AI platforms will operate with greater clarity around permissions. Using them with intentionality, transparency toward your audience, and genuine creative contribution represents a defensible way to participate during this transition period.

## The real stake

The artists who'll genuinely influence AI's trajectory aren't those standing apart from it entirely. They're the ones engaging with it carefully, being honest with audiences, and demanding stronger standards around how these systems work. If you care about how AI should develop — insisting on openness, proper licensing, and authentic human creativity — your influence comes from being part of the discussion, not avoiding it.

Use the tool. Research how it was created. Be open with your audience. Make the creative choices that matter yourself. That constitutes the integrity-based approach to using AI responsibly.
