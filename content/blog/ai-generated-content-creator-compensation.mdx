AI tools trained on millions of artist works without permission now generate content that competes directly with creator income, yet artists whose work fueled that training receive nothing—while major labels collect settlements the original creators never see. The economics are broken, the licensing models are nascent, and **AI compensation for artists** remains one of the most contentious unsolved problems in the creator economy.

We've already explored [how AI training scraped creator content](/blog/how-ai-trained-on-creator-work) without permission. This post digs into what happens next: who profits, who doesn't, and why the money structures being built don't actually reach most artists.

## Why settlements between AI platforms and major labels leave independent artists with nothing

When Universal, Warner, and BMG negotiated licensing agreements with AI music platforms starting in 2024, these deals specifically covered only music catalogs owned by those corporations. Independent creators who release through platforms like DistroKid, TuneCore, or CD Baby had no stake in those negotiations, received no notification that their music was included in AI training datasets, and lacked any mechanism to prevent their work from being used. These agreements prioritize the interests of major label shareholders over the interests of individual songwriters and performers. A studio musician whose vocal performance helped train an AI voice generator receives no payment—despite the fact that their contribution directly made the resulting product possible and now puts future work at financial risk.

The American Federation of Musicians brought legal action on June 5, 2024, challenging the use of musicians' recorded performances in AI training systems and seeking fair compensation for session work.

## How current AI compensation models actually work (and why they fail creators)

The licensing frameworks currently under discussion—including upfront payments for training rights, revenue sharing tied to specific usage, and revenue splits when creators collaborate with AI systems—remain largely experimental or confined to smaller platforms. Real compensation reaching independent creators has been minimal, with the vast majority of individual artists receiving little to nothing from any AI licensing arrangement. The challenge of distributing small payments across millions of creators has made these systems impractical as reliable income sources. Most major platforms are still developing how they'll compensate creators, and few publish transparent information about actual payment amounts.

Three primary compensation structures are being tested or proposed:

- **Training Data Licensing** (companies acquire rights to use existing creative work for model development, paying creators upfront or ongoing fees)
- **Usage-Based Payments** (creators earn money whenever AI systems demonstrably incorporate elements from their work in generated output)
- **Revenue Sharing on Co-Created Work** (when artists partner with AI to make new content, they split the revenue from that content's sale or distribution)

Platforms like Soundverse enable musicians to authorize AI training on their music and receive payments when that work appears in generated content. However, reliably identifying which training sources ended up in which generated outputs remains a complex technical challenge. Scaling this identification across the entire AI music industry presents significant obstacles.

## The attribution problem that makes real compensation impossible

Metadata identifying the original source of training material—including details like artist name, work title, and licensing terms—can be lost or corrupted when audio files are processed, pitch-shifted, tempo-adjusted, or converted between formats. Without required systems for permanently marking original works and maintaining searchable registries of training sources, tracing an AI-generated song back to the human artists whose work trained the model is extremely difficult. The U.S. Copyright Office's 2023 pronouncements on AI focused on clarifying how fair use applies to AI systems, but many core questions about creator rights in AI training remain unsettled in courts. Without working identification systems, enforcing any compensation agreement becomes nearly impossible.

Industry organizations like the RIAA have suggested technical and legal frameworks based on existing standards—similar to how ISRC codes currently identify music recordings. Such a system could theoretically create visibility into training sources and enable fair distribution of compensation, much like how streaming services currently pay royalties based on play counts. However, getting all major AI platforms to adopt and implement such standards remains an unresolved negotiation.

## Why independent artists and advocacy groups want compulsory licensing models

Compulsory licensing would establish a legal requirement that AI platforms pay a fixed percentage of revenue into a collective pool, with distributions calculated based on documented instances of model training on specific artists' work—this approach would mirror how streaming services compensate creators and sidestep the technical challenge of proving specific AI outputs came from specific training sources. The main obstacles to implementing compulsory licensing include creating reliable systems for measuring training use, agreeing on what constitutes a fair payment percentage, and securing cooperation from the major AI platform companies. Most AI companies currently provide minimal transparency regarding what material they trained their systems on.

## What creator income data actually shows about AI's impact

Economic data on the creator economy shows persistent challenges: The majority of people identifying as creators earn substantially less than living wages in their markets, and the potential for AI systems to further reduce demand for human-created music and voice work—particularly affecting session musicians, voice actors, and background performers—represents a genuine economic threat, though we still lack comprehensive studies measuring AI's actual impact on creator earnings to date.

## FAQ: AI compensation for artists, decoded

### What happened in the major label settlements?

Starting in 2024, Universal Music, Warner Music Group, and BMG concluded licensing arrangements with several AI music companies. These agreements created frameworks where AI platforms pay for the ability to train their systems on those labels' music catalogs. Most deals include mechanisms for artists to opt into or out of having their music used for training, though the specific terms and payment details have not been made fully public in most cases.

### Do independent artists get paid from these settlements?

In most cases, no. The settlements specifically protect the interests of the major label corporations. Independent and self-released artists have few options when their music gets incorporated into AI training data under the broad terms most people unknowingly agreed to when signing up for distribution services or social platforms.

### Is there a legal definition of what AI platforms owe artists yet?

The legal framework is still being established. The U.S. Copyright Office has published guidance addressing how fair use relates to AI systems, but fundamental questions about whether training data use constitutes infringement, what compensation creators deserve, and what rights artists should have remain hotly debated and subject to ongoing lawsuits.

### How much do artists actually earn from AI licensing pools?

The amounts vary widely depending on the specific platform. Artists enrolled in platforms that have implemented licensing pools typically receive modest payments, with the actual amount varying based on how often AI systems use their work and the platform's revenue model. Public reporting on typical artist earnings from these programs remains scarce.

### What compensation model do artist advocacy groups actually want?

Artist advocacy organizations consistently push for: complete disclosure of what works were used to train AI systems, required documentation of training datasets, standardized payment rates set by industry agreement or regulation, and systems that ensure payments flow directly to individual creators rather than only to corporate rights holders. The practical challenge of actually building and implementing such systems remains the biggest barrier to change.

## The takeaway

The compensation systems currently under development remain incomplete and favor large rights holders over individual creators. Independent artists lack meaningful options when their work is used to train AI systems without consent or compensation. Establishing fair **AI compensation for artists** requires three fundamental shifts: requiring AI companies to publicly disclose what training sources they used, creating reliable systems to track which training material influenced which generated outputs, and establishing compensation rates and distribution methods that route money to the people who actually created the original work. Until these pieces are substantially in place, creators will continue facing financial harm from AI systems trained on their work.
