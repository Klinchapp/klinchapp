# How to Calculate ROI for AI Coding Tools in Five Steps

To measure the real return on investment from AI coding assistants on your development team, follow five key steps: establish your current spending baseline, quantify actual time freed up per team member, factor in expenses often overlooked during implementation, weigh costs against monthly tool fees, and monitor performance metrics each week. Most organizations achieve positive returns within three to six months when they track results carefully—though vendor promises of 55% productivity improvements often don't hold up when tested against production code.

## How to calculate ROI for AI coding tools in five steps

1. **Document your current costs**: List developer salaries, benefits, and fully-loaded hourly rates. Calculate annual cost per developer (example: $120,000 salary plus 30% benefits equals $156,000 annually, or roughly $75 per hour). Then add up your existing tool investments (IDE subscriptions, code quality tools, deployment infrastructure).

2. **Measure baseline velocity**: Over a one-week period, monitor your team's actual shipping work—pull requests completed, defects resolved, new capabilities delivered, or whatever your organization tracks best. This gives you your starting point. Also log the hours your team spends reviewing and validating code.

3. **Add AI tool subscriptions**: Consider GitHub Copilot ($10–20/month), Cursor ($20/month), or comparable AI-assisted development platforms. Calculate real-world expenses carefully; published rates often underestimate actual spending once you factor in all usage tiers and premium features.

4. **Run a 4-week pilot**: Introduce the tool to a small group of 2-3 developers working on representative work. Measure the same metrics you tracked initially. Be sure to subtract the extra time reviewers spend examining, improving, or security-checking AI-generated code.

5. **Calculate your breakeven point**: If your team gains back 5 hours per developer per week (a sensible mid-range figure from field studies), that's 5 × $75 = $375 in weekly value. With $50/month per developer in subscription fees, you reach breakeven in roughly 1–2 weeks. However, if the actual gain is only 1–2 hours per week, you won't break even for 6–8 weeks—or may not at all.

## Why measured time savings vary so much between teams

**Real productivity gains depend heavily on your specific codebase, the expertise of your developers, and how thoroughly you count all the costs.** Vendors frequently advertise 30–55% speed increases, yet findings from real organizations paint a more nuanced picture. Research examining how developers actually use these tools shows that outcomes fluctuate considerably, and the gap between what vendors promise and what teams observe in practice is often substantial.

Time gains differ by the kind of work being done: repetitive tasks like standard API wrappers, database functions, and boilerplate testing see meaningful improvements. Novel work requiring creative problem-solving or sophisticated architectural thinking shows smaller gains because these tools work better with familiar patterns. Your less-experienced developers will probably gain more benefit than your most senior engineers—research indicates that newcomers to development tend to see bigger improvements than veterans.

Additional expenses chip away at improvements significantly. Every code snippet generated by AI needs someone to verify it works correctly. Security reviews take extra time when machine-generated code reaches your production systems. Quality suffers if you prioritize speed over correctness, creating accumulated technical debt. What organizations have found is that while AI tools can increase lines of code or features shipped, the real time savings per task may be modest once you account for quality checks and testing that shift into later stages.

## How to account for costs most teams ignore

**Setup effort, expanded code review workload, and additional security work represent the three biggest hidden costs—and they typically eat up 30–50% of the time savings AI supposedly provides.** Getting your team comfortable with the tools takes 2–4 weeks. Setting up usage policies, permitted tools, and spending guardrails requires another 1–2 weeks of work. Your accounting and operations teams now need to monitor AI spending, meaning regular usage reviews and the potential for unexpected costs when usage spikes.

Code review becomes more demanding because your team has to evaluate both human-written and machine-generated code for correctness. Security assessments expand because AI-written code introduces patterns your security team may not have seen before or thoroughly validated. Well-established teams have found that real productivity gains only emerge after they've fine-tuned their whole development workflow—their testing, CI/CD pipelines, and review systems. If your organization already struggles with code review delays or testing backlogs, adding AI won't solve those problems; it will highlight them.

Subscription costs differ across platforms. GitHub Copilot uses straightforward monthly pricing ($10–20/month). Competing products often bill based on actual usage or token consumption, which creates variability depending on how much developers actually use the features.

## Which teams should adopt AI coding tools now

**Independent contractors and young companies focused on building infrastructure quickly (connecting systems, building standard database operations, generating tests) achieve positive returns fast. Large organizations with sophisticated code review processes and testing frameworks typically see returns in 2–3 months. Organizations working with older systems or solving difficult technical problems should expect a longer evaluation period before seeing clear financial benefits.**

Adoption makes immediate sense if your team dedicates >30% of their time to routine coding tasks. If your work centers on solving new problems, fixing systems you didn't create, or making architectural improvements, financial benefits are less clear and require hands-on testing first. Senior developers may see limited improvements unless they regularly work on standard infrastructure tasks. Junior and mid-level developers typically see better results.

The crucial element is keeping honest track of what changes. Record your team's weekly output before and after the rollout. If you're not saving enough time to outweigh the subscription and operational costs within two months, stop and investigate what's happening—commonly, your team isn't doing the kinds of work these tools are built for, or your review process is requiring more time than anticipated.
